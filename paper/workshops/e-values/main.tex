\begin{abstract}
Measuring correlated failure between language models requires a null hypothesis for
``independent enough,'' and prior work shows the conclusion is highly sensitive to which null is
chosen, leaving the choice apparently subjective. We show one null is not a choice: the row and
column margins of a model-by-item outcome matrix are the jointly sufficient statistics of the
Rasch family, so the uniform distribution over margin-matched matrices is the unique fit-free
conditional null for that family. At this null, on $1{,}228$--$1{,}362$ open models across five
benchmarks, the commonly reported mean-level ``excess'' co-failure is a deterministic function of
item difficulty and vanishes identically, while a second-order residual correlation of
$2.9$--$11.9\times$ its null survives, is robust to removing $21$--$64\%$ of models as
near-duplicates, and concentrates in $4$--$12$ latent dimensions rather than one shared factor.
Our test is a fixed-sample exact randomization test. Because open leaderboards grow
continuously, the natural downstream use is a standing monitor, which is an anytime-valid inference
problem. We state what the exact null already gives for that purpose (a valid per-look $p$-value,
hence calibrated e-values that merge validly across a fixed set of looks) and what it does not (an
e-process with optional stopping), and pose the missing construction as an open problem for this
workshop rather than as a result.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

If many deployed models share blind spots, an ensemble, a panel of model judges, or a market of
competing screening vendors supplies far less independent evidence than its headcount suggests.
A growing literature measures how often models err together \citep{kim2025}, almost all of it
by comparing the rate at which models fail the same items against a baseline in which failures
are independent. \citet{jo2026} show this comparison is highly sensitive to the
assumed baseline: conditioning on item difficulty attenuates residual correlations, ``some even
flipping from strongly positive to slightly negative,'' and they prove that a sufficiently
expressive null can explain away any observed dependence. Their stated conclusion is that the
choice of null is subjective. This paper argues that the ladder of nulls has a canonical rung,
reports what the exact answer is there, and states precisely what that answer does and does not
provide for the sequential-monitoring problem this workshop's machinery addresses.

\section{The canonical null}

Let $F \in \{0,1\}^{N\times M}$ record $F_{im}=1$ iff model $i$ fails item $m$, with row sums
$r_i$ and column sums $c_m$. The Rasch model $\Pr(F_{im}=1)=\sigma(\alpha_i+\beta_m)$ has
$\{r_i\}$ and $\{c_m\}$ as jointly sufficient statistics, so the conditional law of $F$ given its
margins is uniform on the set of margin-matched binary matrices for every parameter value
\citep{rasch1960,andersen1973}. That set is the ecologists' fixed-fixed null. We sample it with
curveball \citep{strona2014}, whose convergence to the uniform distribution on the fibre is proved
by \citet{carstens2015} provided failed trades are counted as steps (ours does), and which we
verify directly: enumerating every matrix on four small fibres (sizes $48$, $90$, $204$, $1{,}170$)
and drawing $60{,}000$ thinned samples, a $\chi^2$ test against uniformity is not rejected on any
($p=0.23, 0.05, 0.73, 0.15$); on the real matrices, four dispersed chains give Gelman--Rubin
$\hat R\in[0.98,1.03]$ at the burn-in used. Unlike a fitted item-response null, this null requires
no optimizer, no penalty, and no identification convention: within the Rasch family it is the
strongest ability-and-difficulty control obtainable without estimating anything.

\begin{lemma}[Mean co-failure depends only on item margins]
\label{lem:mean}
The mean pairwise co-failure rate satisfies
$O=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac1M\sum_m F_{im}F_{jm}=\frac{1}{MN(N-1)}\sum_m (c_m^2 - c_m)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\sum_{i\neq j}F_{im}F_{jm}=(\sum_i F_{im})^2-\sum_i F_{im}^2=c_m^2-c_m$ since $F_{im}\in\{0,1\}$.
\end{proof}
\noindent Any randomization preserving the item margins therefore leaves $O$ invariant with
exactly zero variance, so the ``excess'' of $O$ over this null is identically zero by
construction rather than empirically small. The same degeneracy is known in ecology as that of
Schluter's V-ratio \citep{schluter1984,gotelli2000}, in psychometrics as Cronbach's $\alpha$
\citep{cronbach1951}, and in inter-rater agreement as Fleiss's observed agreement
\citep{fleiss1971}; to our knowledge none of these is cited in the model-evaluation literature that
reports the statistic, and the full paper states and attributes the identities.

\section{What survives at the exact null}
\label{sec:survives}

\paragraph{Statistics.} Let $P_{im}=\sigma(\hat\alpha_i+\hat\beta_m)$ be the maximum-entropy
(Rasch) mean field matching the observed margins, fitted to a maximum absolute margin error below
$10^{-11}$. It is a point estimate of the null mean of $FF^{\!\top}/M$, and we validate it against
the randomization itself: over $319{,}600$ model pairs on ARC, the curveball expectation of
$C_{ij}=\frac1M\sum_m F_{im}F_{jm}$ and the Rasch prediction correlate at $0.99999$. The residual
matrix is $D=(FF^{\!\top}-PP^{\!\top})/M$ and $R$ is $D$ scaled to unit diagonal. We report (i)
the root-mean-square off-diagonal $|R_{ij}|$ and (ii) the participation ratio
$\mathrm{PR}=(\sum_k\lambda_k^{+})^2/\sum_k(\lambda_k^{+})^2$ of $R$'s positive eigenvalues, each
against the same quantity on exact-margin curveball replicates, and (iii) the number of
eigenvalues of $R$ exceeding the null's largest eigenvalue (parallel analysis against the exact
null rather than against iid noise).

\paragraph{Data.} Per-item outcomes are harvested from the archived Open LLM Leaderboard
($1{,}228$--$1{,}362$ models per benchmark after removing degenerate rows and columns; five
benchmarks) at zero inference cost by reading only the metric column of each result file. Item
identity is resolved by row index, licensed by a verified zero-mismatch row-order audit over $334$
models spanning the archive's two schema generations.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:neff} shows residual correlation at $2.9$--$11.9\times$ its
null and a participation ratio at $2.8$--$21.4\%$ of its null on every benchmark. Neither number
licenses the reading ``$1{,}362$ models behave like $24$'': for the unclipped spectrum,
$\mathrm{PR}=N/(1+(N-1)\,\overline{R_{ij}^2})$ exactly ($16.04$ on ARC computed either way), so
PR is a monotone transform of mean squared residual correlation and cannot separate one weak
global factor from a partition into tight clusters. The diagnostics that can separate them say the
structure is neither: on ARC the leading eigenvalue carries $54\%$ of squared residual mass (a
single factor would carry $\approx99\%$, twenty exact clone blocks $\approx10\%$), deflating it
raises PR only to $46.8$ rather than toward $N$, and six eigenvalues exceed the null's spectral
edge of $27.9$. A two-parameter item-response simulation with discrimination heterogeneity of
$\mathrm{sd}(\log a)=0.60$ produces rms residual correlation of $0.076$ against the observed
$0.248$, so discrimination heterogeneity alone is not a sufficient explanation, while a simulated
population with two ability dimensions and no shared lineage reproduces much of the effect
($\lambda_1^2/\sum\lambda_k^2=0.457$ against the observed $0.540$).

\paragraph{Robustness.} Recomputing every statistic on populations deduplicated at agreement
threshold $0.95$, which discards $21$--$64\%$ of models, with the Rasch fit and the null redrawn
each time, moves the rms ratio by at most $1.14\times$ (ARC $5.58\to5.44$) and leaves the
eigenvalue count unchanged on three of five benchmarks; the PR ratio is the one statistic that
moves materially, by $2.44\times$ on HellaSwag and by at most $1.46\times$ elsewhere. A later pass of the identical estimator
over the growing archive ($2{,}972$--$3{,}762$ models per benchmark, $2.2$--$2.8\times$ the
population here) moved every PR ratio down, by $0.005$--$0.010$ on four benchmarks and by $0.023$
on GSM8K. On the independently implemented successor harness (ARC, $212$ models) the rms excess is
$4.1\times$ its null with four eigenvalues above the edge, agreeing in direction but not in
magnitude. On planted data ($400$ models, $700$ items, $20$ datasets per condition), the test
reaches power $1.00$ against shared failure modes at a planted rms of $0.048$ and rejects at
$0.017$ under the null; against the alternative in which every model shares one item-difficulty
profile and differs only in ability, power is exactly $\alpha$, because that alternative is the
null by construction.

\paragraph{A refuted pre-registration.} The pre-registered primary statistic was the dispersion
$\operatorname{Var}_{i<j}(C_{ij})$. It came out below its null on three benchmarks and above on
two, its kill condition fired, and we report it as refuted rather than reinterpreted. The
second-order structure above is what survives, not the statistic we planned to report.

\section{What the exact null gives, and does not give, for anytime-valid monitoring}
\label{sec:evalues}

Everything above is a fixed-$N$ exact test. The population is not fixed: the leaderboard and its
successors add models continuously, and the natural downstream use of the test is a standing
monitor (``has correlated failure in the open ecosystem worsened since last quarter?'') checked
repeatedly as submissions arrive, without the type-I inflation that repeated testing on a growing
sample otherwise incurs. That is the problem this community formalizes with e-values and test
martingales \citep{shafer2021,vovkwang2021,ramdas2023,grunwald2024}.

\paragraph{What is already in hand.} At any population size $N_t$ the null law of the accumulated
matrix given its current margins is exactly uniform on a finite set, so the Monte Carlo $p$-value
$p_t$ at each look is exactly valid. Two consequences follow directly from the e-value literature.
Any single $p_t$ can be calibrated into an e-value, for instance $\kappa p_t^{\kappa-1}$ with
$\kappa\in(0,1)$ \citep{vovkwang2021}; and the arithmetic mean of e-values is an e-value under
arbitrary dependence \citep{vovkwang2021}, so averaging calibrated e-values over a pre-specified
set of monitoring dates is a valid combined test across those looks even though nested populations
are strongly dependent. What this does not give is optional stopping: it is valid for a fixed set
of looks, not for stopping the first time the running evidence looks large, which is the guarantee
an e-process provides \citep{ramdas2023}.

\paragraph{What is missing, and why.} A test martingale needs a null for the transition from the
population at time $t$ to that at $t+1$. Conditioning on the margins of the accumulated matrix
eliminates the Rasch parameters at each fixed $t$, but the margins themselves change when a row is
added, and the conditional argument does not supply the null distribution of that increment: given
the first $N_t$ rows and the new margins, the new row is determined. The sequential object must
therefore be built differently, for instance by conditioning each arriving row on its own row sum
(which eliminates that model's ability parameter but not the item parameters) and handling the
item parameters as a composite nuisance with a mixture or plug-in e-value over the evidence
accumulated about them, at the cost of the exactness that motivates the fixed-$N$ test. A second
obstacle is selection: new models are not exchangeable arrivals but are submitted by people who
observe the current leaderboard, which is the population-dependence objection \citet{jo2026} raise
about a fixed null, now posed sequentially. We state both as the concrete open problem we would
bring to this workshop, not as a result. The exact conditional null is, we think, the right
fixed-sample primitive for an anytime-valid ecosystem monitor; we have not built the monitor.

\section{Limitations}

Claims cover the evaluated open-model ecosystem on multiple-choice-style benchmarks, not deployed
proprietary systems, and the population is self-selected leaderboard submissions. The conditioning
model is unidimensional; Section~\ref{sec:survives} rules out discrimination heterogeneity and
duplicate models as sufficient explanations but cannot separate genuine behavioural concentration
from an underfit null family, and on the spectral evidence the latter is the better description.
Two candid points about the null itself: its rms sits only $1.25$--$2.0\times$ above the analytic
$1/\sqrt{M}$ floor, and conditioning on the item margins alone reproduces it to within $8.3\%$ on
every benchmark, so the item margins do essentially all of the conditioning work for this
statistic. Code, the harmonised substrate, and every results artifact are archived at
\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21860005}{doi:10.5281/zenodo.21860005}
(\url{https://github.com/SilverCoin256/cofail}); the pre-registration with dated amendments is at
\url{https://osf.io/cmh7q/}. A large language model (Claude, Anthropic) was used as a coding and
drafting assistant; the author specified and pre-registered the hypotheses, verified every
derivation and every reported number against executed-run artifacts, and is responsible for the
content.
